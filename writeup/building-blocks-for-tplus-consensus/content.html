How Tplus’s First Releases Will Deal with Consensus

As any TEE-based technology that cares about security and safety at tplus we’ve been exploring different approaches to tackle both integrity and confidentiality in case hardware RoT isn’t enough, but also around solving availability. Going forward, maybe by beta release, we’ll likely be introducing dynamic quorums into tplus (even if there’s some nuance there due to the local choices nature of FBA which we can’t enforce as the higher authority is a l1 smart contract), but for now the approach we’ve taken towards these two problems is simlper and serves as building blocks for future improvements.

It’s worth it to note that for these initial experimental releases we grant complete trust over the TEE’s ability to maintain integrity and confidentiality, an assumption which we won’t be making in production (and possibly beta).

Slot Finalization and Checkpointing

Similarly to blockchains, tplus has a concept of slot finalization and checkpointing:

  • slot finalization is a commit from the leader publishing the set of actions that brought their internal state to change.
  • checkpoints are a record of the internal state that are gossiped and potentially kept outside of a tee.

Slots in tplus can be compared to slots in blockchains, while checkpoints are a record to keep off-chain that can be decrypted only by a select council in case of a full system restart considering tplus operates fully in (encrypted) memory.

The workflow is roughly:

  1. Leader peer receives actions from clients mutating internal state.
  2. At slot finalization (currently time-based) these actions are batched and forwarded to the backup peers.
  3. The backup peers are dormient, i.e they will ignore any message that mutates state which isn’t the slot finalization from the leader. When receiving the slot they’ll execute the actions mutating their internal state.
  4. Every \(n\) slot finalizations the leader will also publish a checkpoint, which contains a copy of the internal state’s critical elements (finalized inventories). Checkpoints are mapped to their hash, and are encrypted to key shards that are encrypted to the council’s members.
  5. Checkpoint instructions (not the full contents) are included within the slot’s actions, allowing backup peers to endorse the checkpoints as well. How to leverage endorsements for alpha is still WIP, there’s various approaches that we can take on that front. For beta or prod we’ll likely leverage the dynamic quorum.

If the full system goes down (e.g network-wide bug, DoS, etc) the checkpoints can be decrypted by the council within a TEE by encrypting the key share to the TEE’s attestation-endorsed pubkey, meaning that unless the threshold of council members colludes inventories remain encrypted. Once the inventories are decrypted within the TEE such box can become the leader box again by submitting proof that they’re running tplus software and that their internal state matches the one of a checkpoint. The L1 smart contract might also carry some checks over the checkpoint’s endorsement (e.g is it endorsed by at least \(n\) TEEs?).

Vertical Architecture

Tplus’ architecture is heavily modular and as a result there’s some peers that execute different software than others and have different levels of importance: the core of the product, the clearing engine (CE), is the most important peer type, then we have the orderbooks (OB) that enable orders to get matched (OB is subordinate to CE) and then we have the order management system (OMS) which is the interface for everyday users to manage all their orders across the various books (OMS is subordinate to OB and CE).

Given we have node types (downstream nodes) that trigger actions on upstream ones and depend on their internal state mutations reflecting such actions, we want downstream nodes to be able to tell whether their actions were indeed accepted by upstream or if they were lost in networking (packet loss, connection errors, censorship, etc). Downstream can use slot finalizations to enforce this:

  1. Downstream sends actions to upstream.

  2. Upstream notifies downstream that the action is in slotid (where slotid is made of a nonce and a unique random session key).

  3. When slot slotid finalizes the slot is broadcasted by upstream leader to its peers, each broadcasting an acknowledgement over such slotid.

  4. Downstream subscribes to acknowledgement topic and if it gathers enough acknowledgements it can reasonably assume that slotid is replicated across upstream peers. So even if upstream leader goes down downstream has guarantees around their action being part of the new upstream leader’s state.

    • one technique for rotating CE upstream leader if previous leader is unresponsive is to have each peer share with L1 contract their latest ingested finalized slot, the one from which has the latest nonce with leader’s session key will be the new leader.

Please note that some of these mechanisms rely on the fact that integrity across peers is guaranteed by the TEE, in fact L1 contract won’t enable to form dynamic quorums and check over endorsements in the checkpoint.
